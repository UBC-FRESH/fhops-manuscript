\section{Illustrative example}
\label{sec:illustrative-example}

This section evaluates FHOPS on the public BC ground-based ladder (tiny7, small21, med42) plus synthetic scaling tiers. In this ladder, horizons increase from 7 to 21 to 42 days and block counts from 2 to 6 to 12 with the same 9-machine role mix. The goal is a reproducible baseline for practitioner interpretation and follow-on modelling studies, consistent with review-identified reproducibility gaps \cite{jaffray2026systematic}.

We organize the results around three operational questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What quality-runtime trade-offs are observed across solver families?
  \item How robust are resulting schedules under operational disturbances?
  \item What cost and scale signals are visible from the current baseline?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Operational question 1: quality-runtime trade-offs across solver families}
Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} summarizes SA, ILS, and Tabu outcomes by scenario. Three patterns are relevant for planning decisions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small instance (tiny7):} objectives are tightly grouped (4295.77--4338.52) and production is identical (4414.70~m$^3$), while runtime spans 25.13--245.50~s.
  \item \textbf{Intermediate instance (small21):} objective spread remains narrow (15584.16--15605.94) with identical production (15966.97~m$^3$), but runtime spans 53.06--315.61~s and mobilisation cost spans 1764.12--1871.80~CAD.
  \item \textbf{Larger instance in this ladder (med42):} objectives are positive and close (24130.32 for SA, 24163.37 for Tabu, and 24329.17 for ILS, the best med42 score; a spread of 198.85), but KPI ordering is not uniform across objective, production, runtime, and mobilisation: objective ILS $>$ Tabu $>$ SA, production Tabu $>$ ILS $>$ SA (33463.63, 33361.75, and 32984.91~m$^3$), runtime SA $<$ ILS $<$ Tabu (911.22, 1212.70, and 5387.72~s), and mobilisation cost SA $<$ ILS $<$ Tabu. Solver selection should therefore follow planning priorities rather than one headline score. None of the three plans incurs a hard-violation penalty: each objective equals delivered production minus undelivered block volume minus half the mobilisation cost (for SA, $32984.91-5208.32-0.5\times7292.52=24130.32$).
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:tuning} adds parameter-search evidence. Each row reports the best tuner, its tuning budget, and $\Delta$, the best tuned objective minus the objective of the SA default preset in Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}. The SA default runs with a much larger budget than a tuning run (8000, 4000, 20000, and 6000 SA iterations for tiny7, small21, med42, and synthetic-small), so $\Delta$ compares a short tuning run with a full benchmark run rather than two equal-effort searches. Reported $\Delta$ values are 42.74 (tiny7), 0.14 (small21), $-990.50$ (med42), and 0.00 (synthetic-small). On tiny7 the tuned score (4338.52) matches the ILS and Tabu results of Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks}; on small21 the difference of 0.14 is effectively a tie; on med42 the best tuner (Tabu, one run of 900 iterations) reaches 23139.82, 4.1\% below the SA default (24130.32), so under the maximisation convention the parameter search did not beat the default within its budget; synthetic-small is unchanged. Tuning impact is therefore scenario- and budget-dependent, which reinforces that tuning results should be interpreted per scenario rather than as a uniform improvement \cite{nelson2003forestmodels}.

In synthetic-small no volume is delivered within the horizon, so its score of $-39.79$ is the unmet-volume term of the objective ($\omega_{\text{prod}}=1.0$), identical for all three heuristics.

\subsection{Operational question 2: schedule robustness under disturbance}
Deterministic schedules are not sufficient for operational adoption, so leading schedules are replayed under deterministic and stochastic conditions. Because operational, mechanical, and weather delays strongly affect delivered production and harvesting cost, stochastic playback keeps the solved schedule fixed and samples three disturbance types: machine downtime (a scheduled machine-shift loses a sampled number of hours, with a mean of 4~h and a standard deviation of 1.5~h), weather (production on affected days is reduced by a sampled severity), and landing shocks (production at an affected landing is reduced by a sampled factor for a fixed number of days). The manuscript runs use 50 samples per schedule, with event probabilities of 0.05 per scheduled machine-shift (downtime), 0.10 per day (weather), and 0.05 per landing and day (landing shocks).

Figure~\ref{fig:playback} shows that disturbance sensitivity is scenario-dependent, while differences between SA and ILS are negligible: their deterministic utilization is identical in each scenario, and their stochastic means differ by less than 0.001. Average day-level utilization changes from 0.365 (deterministic) to 0.362 (stochastic) in tiny7 and from 0.566 to 0.562 in med42, for both SA and ILS. Synthetic-small shows the largest drop (0.378 to 0.369). Utilization measures worked hours, so it responds to downtime only; weather and landing shocks reduce production rather than worked hours and are reflected in delivered production rather than in utilization. This reinforces a practical selection rule: schedule quality should be judged jointly by objective value and robustness metrics, not objective value alone.

\subsection{Operational question 3: cost interpretation and scaling behavior}
Cost summaries complement utilization and production metrics by translating schedule effects into operating implications. On med42, mobilization cost in Table~\ref{tab:benchmarks} spans 7292.52--9141.32~CAD across solver choices, while objectives differ by less than 1\%, indicating that comparable schedule quality can imply different movement-cost profiles. In this baseline, costing is used for within-study comparison, not external economic extrapolation.

Scaling results from synthetic tiers (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}) show increasing runtime under fixed solver budgets: 20.69~s (small, 4 blocks), 45.86~s (medium, 8 blocks), and 69.22~s (large, 16 blocks). The value is a reproducible scale signal measured in the same platform used for benchmark and robustness reporting.

\subsection{Scope boundaries and interpretation limits}
Three limits are essential for correct use of this section:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The public ladder currently represents one BC ground-based archetype; broader system families remain out of scope.
  \item Objective values are comparable within scenario only; cross-scenario interpretation should rely on secondary KPIs (e.g., production, utilization, cost) and relative trends.
  \item Absolute runtime depends on hardware and solver budgets; comparisons in this manuscript are most reliable as within-study baselines.
\end{itemize}
